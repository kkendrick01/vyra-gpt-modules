SECTION 2

The AM's Responsibility for Culture

AMs shape internal culture in four primary ways. Your job is not to be nice. Your job is to create an environment where people can do their best work reliably, predictably, and sustainably.

1

You Set the Tone

Your language, pace, clarity, and presence determine how others show up. If you're rushed and unclear, the team becomes rushed and unclear.

2

You Establish Expectations

If you set unclear or unrealistic expectations, culture fractures. If you communicate structure, culture strengthens.

3

You Control Information Flow

Your summaries, briefs, updates, and recaps create shared understanding. Information gaps create confusion and conflict.

4

You Create Emotional Safety

People perform better when they feel respected, supported, and valued. Psychological safety is a performance multiplier.

SECTION 4

Communication Standards Inside DL

Communication is the backbone of culture. Your communication signals your leadership capacity. Every message you send builds or erodes trust.

1. The Structured Message Rule

Every substantive message must include four elements:

Purpose Context Request Deadline

2. The "No Orphans" Rule

No task is left ownerless. Every task has an assigned person and a due date. Unassigned work creates confusion and dropped balls.

3. The Confirmation Rule

Every important message receives acknowledgment within a reasonable timeframe. Silence creates uncertainty.

4. The Recap Rule

After every significant conversation, the AM summarizes decisions clearly. Written recaps prevent misunderstanding.

5. The Emotional Neutrality Rule

Emotional regulation is required. Messages must not show frustration or panic. Calm is contagious—so is chaos.

At DL, communication must be: organized, concise, calm, complete, contextual, professional, timely, and respectful.

SECTION 10

Culture Scenarios and Practice Drills

These drills build muscle memory. Practice handling internal situations with the frameworks you've learned.

01

The Frustrated Writer

A writer pushes back on a brief, saying it's unclear and they don't understand what you want. Their tone is frustrated. You can tell they feel set up to fail.

EXPERT APPROACH

First, own it: "I hear you—that brief wasn't clear enough, and that's on me. Let me fix it." Don't get defensive. Their frustration is valid if the direction was vague.

Then clarify: "Here's what I'm looking for: [specific outcome]. The emotional tone should feel [X]. The angle is [Y]. Does this make more sense?"

Reset expectations: "Going forward, if a brief isn't landing, flag it immediately. I'd rather clarify early than have you spin. We're on the same team."

02

Inconsistent Communication Pattern

You notice that a recurring problem keeps appearing: information gets lost between you, the writer, and the editor. No one seems to have the full picture. Deadlines slip. Confusion spreads.

EXPERT APPROACH

Audit the flow: Where exactly does information drop off? Is it in the brief? In the handoff to editing? In feedback loops? Map the actual sequence of events for the last 3 problems.

Implement structure: Create a single source of truth—a shared document or thread where all decisions live. Every handoff includes: objective, context, deadline, owner.

Communicate the change: "I'm implementing a new system so we all have the same information. Every brief will follow this structure, and all updates go here. Let me know if anything is unclear."

03

A Misaligned Handoff

Content came back from the writer and it missed the mark. The editor caught it, but now there's a revision cycle that shouldn't have happened. The handoff was the problem.

EXPERT APPROACH

New handoff structure:

Objective: What this content needs to accomplish in one sentence.

Narrative tie-in: How this connects to the client's broader positioning.

Angle: The specific theme or point of view.

Tone: The emotional register (aspirational, tactical, vulnerable, etc.).

Story materials: Specific quotes, anecdotes, or examples to use.

What success looks like: One example of content that hit the mark.

04

Client Request That Strains the Team

The client wants 6 pieces of content in a week when the normal cadence is 4. They have a product launch. You know the team is already at capacity.

EXPERT APPROACH

Don't just say no. Offer options: "I understand this launch is important. Here's what we can do: Option A—we deliver 4 pieces at our usual quality and prioritize the highest-impact angles. Option B—we deliver 6, but I need to flag that quality may be compressed given the timeline."

Be direct: "My recommendation is Option A. Quality matters more than quantity for your brand, especially during a launch. What matters most to you this week?"

Protect the team silently. Don't dump the pressure on them. Manage the client expectation so the team can focus.

05

Misunderstanding Between Writer and Editor

The editor gave feedback the writer disagrees with. There's tension. Both feel they're right. You're caught in the middle.

EXPERT APPROACH

Get on a quick call with both. Don't take sides. Start with: "Let's figure out what we're optimizing for on this piece." Return to the brief and the client's objective.

Facilitate clarity: "Writer, walk me through your thinking. Editor, walk me through yours." Often the disagreement is about interpretation of the goal, not the actual work.

Make the call: "Based on the objective, here's the direction we'll go. Let's document this so we're aligned for next time." Then move on. Don't let small conflicts linger.
